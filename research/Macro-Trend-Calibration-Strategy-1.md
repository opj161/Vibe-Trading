### **Concrete Parameterizations in Benchmark Indices and Leading CTAs**

### **The Universal Parameter Assumption**

A critical finding in institutional trend-following research is that leading CTAs generally do not curve-fit different moving average lengths to different asset classes. Instead, they apply a universal set of lookback parameters across all markets—equities, gold, bonds, and FX—relying on daily volatility scaling to normalize the data inputs3.  
The academic literature demonstrates that when daily returns are scaled by their inverse ex-ante volatility (typically a 60-day or 65-day exponentially weighted moving average of squared returns), the underlying trend characteristics become mathematically comparable across asset classes17. Therefore, a 12-month lookback applied to a volatility-scaled US Treasury future functions with the same statistical efficacy as a 12-month lookback applied to volatility-scaled gold. Attempting to optimize a specific moving average pair for gold (e.g., a 45-day breakout) versus a different pair for equities (e.g., a 200-day moving average) is widely considered by institutional quants to introduce severe look-ahead bias and overfitting, degrading out-of-sample robustness8.

## **Multi-Horizon Ensembles and the Redundancy of the Medium Term**

A foundational tenet of modern systematic trading is "time-scale diversification"—the practice of combining fast, medium, and slow trend signals to smooth returns, limit drawdown severity, and increase statistical robustness33. Historically, the industry allocated capital relatively equally across these horizons under the assumption that all assets benefit equally from all speeds. The 60-to-120 day lookback window was universally heralded as the "sweet spot" of trend following4.  
However, cutting-edge quantitative research published in late 2025 and 2026 by researchers affiliated with Ai For Alpha, Centrale Lyon, and Université Paris Dauphine fundamentally challenges this heuristic. In a landmark Bayesian optimization study ("Revisiting the Structure of Trend Premia: When Diversification Hides Redundancy"), researchers evaluated the explicit value of layering multiple lookback horizons (20, 60, 125, 250, and 500 days)33.  
The findings present a critical paradigm shift for macro signal calibration. The researchers demonstrated that the medium-term component (specifically the 125-day horizon) is empirically and mathematically redundant once short-term (20-60 day) and long-term (250-500 day) signals are included in the portfolio33. Through a minimum-variance formulation, the study proved that the medium-term signal acts merely as a linear combination of its adjacent horizons, meaning it provides overlapping noise rather than unique alpha34.  
By explicitly removing the 125-day layer from the ensemble, the researchers found that Sharpe ratios and drawdown efficiencies strictly improved, while benchmark correlation was maintained33. The optimal modern macro trend signal adopts a "barbell" structure. The short-term horizon (20–60 days) provides essential convexity and reactivity during sudden crisis events, while the ultra-long-term horizon (250–500 days) captures persistent macroeconomic shifts and ignores mean-reverting noise33.  
For a system architect, attempting to blend a crypto-tuned ![][image1] into a macro ensemble fails not only because it is too fast on an absolute basis, but because placing it alongside a medium-term signal creates structural redundancy. Moving directly to a Fast/Slow barbell (e.g., an equally risk-weighted blend of a 40-day breakout and a 250-day breakout) represents the most empirically robust frontier in contemporary CTA design.

## **Cross-Asset Diversification: Digital Assets vs. Traditional Macro**

Given the user's success in digital asset trend-following, a critical portfolio construction question emerges: does crypto trend-following offer genuine cross-asset diversification against macro trend-following, particularly during risk-off events?  
Recent quantitative analyses demonstrate that trend-following on digital assets operates almost entirely independently of traditional macro trend-following, offering profound diversification benefits.  
Historical backtests comparing tactical trend-following on a basket of leading cryptocurrencies against the SG Trend Index reveal an average rolling correlation of just 7.4% over the past decade2. While this correlation occasionally oscillates to 30% during periods of immense global liquidity injections, it frequently touches negative territory2. Analysis by Two Sigma utilizing their Factor Lens confirms this decoupling, attributing only 9% of Bitcoin's total risk to traditional macroeconomic factors, with only a minor positive loading on the traditional trend-following factor1.  
The mechanisms driving this decoupling are rooted in market microstructure. Crypto markets are driven by high retail participation, technological adoption cycles, sentiment-driven herding, and extreme speculative leverage1. Conversely, traditional macro trends are dictated by central bank policy, global GDP growth, institutional hedging flows, and geopolitical supply chain shocks. Because the underlying behavioral drivers of the trends are entirely disparate, the signals naturally decouple.  
However, the concept of "crisis alpha" must be nuanced when discussing digital assets. A genuine diversification benefit exists if an asset class generates outsized returns during equity drawdowns. Traditional CTAs explicitly provide this because they dynamically short risk assets (equities, corporate credit) and go long safe havens (government bonds, USD) when macroeconomic fear spikes18.  
Crypto trend-following generates absolute return through massive, independent parabolic moves rather than structurally shorting the global economy. During acute liquidity shocks—such as the March 2020 COVID crash—both Bitcoin and traditional equities plummeted simultaneously1. Digital assets remain heavily correlated to the macro "risk-on/risk-off" dynamic during these panic events1. Therefore, while crypto trend-following is an unparalleled absolute return generator, it is not a reliable source of traditional macro crisis alpha.  
Running a fast, volatility-scaled crypto system alongside a slow, barbell-structured macro system creates a highly synergistic, non-correlated ensemble. This architecture vastly improves the overall portfolio Sharpe ratio, provided the macro sleeve is relied upon to handle the heavy defensive lifting during global deflationary shocks.

## **Realistic Performance Benchmarks and Expectations**

When building a macro trend system, backtests that exhibit steady 45-degree upward equity curves are universally overfit. Realistic macro trend-following is characterized by long periods of sideways churn—often lasting 18 to 24 months—punctuated by sharp, convex gains during macroeconomic dislocations18.  
Based on verified data from the SG Trend Index, the Barclay BTOP50 Index, and institutional research spanning decades, a properly built macro trend system must be calibrated against the following realistic priors before any backtesting is finalized:

### **Net Sharpe Ratio Expectations**

Credible, long-term net-of-fee Sharpe ratios for diversified macro trend systems hover between 0.40 and 0.6020. Pre-cost (gross) Sharpe ratios historically range from 0.70 to 0.9020. Any macro backtest claiming a Sharpe ratio greater than 1.2 over a 20-year period across standard liquid futures is almost certainly suffering from look-ahead bias, improper transaction cost modeling, or overfitting to a specific macroeconomic regime.

### **Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)**

Since its inception in 2000, the SG Trend Index has delivered a historical CAGR of approximately 5.33% to 5.64%28. The broader BTOP50 Index has produced a historical CAGR of 4.08%28. While systematic programs target a 10% to 12% annualized volatility, the gross returns are significantly reduced by the substantial drag of transaction costs, slippage, and futures curve roll yields.

### **Win Rate and Skewness**

Trend-following relies entirely on positive skew rather than accuracy. Credible systems operate with win rates between 35% and 45%37. The mathematical edge requires cutting losing trades ruthlessly—often taking consecutive paper cuts during choppy markets—while allowing rare, massive winners to run to completion. The return distribution of a CTA is highly positively skewed, functioning similarly to holding a straddle option position21.

### **Maximum Drawdown Profile**

Drawdowns are an inherent, structural feature of trend-following, not a systemic failure. Since 2000, the SG Trend Index has suffered 16 drawdowns greater than 10%, with a maximum historical drawdown of \-20.61%18. A robust system targeting 10% annualized volatility must expect periodic drawdowns in the 15% to 25% range, particularly during prolonged macro consolidations or abrupt "V-shaped" central bank interventions.

## **Beyond Moving Averages: Advanced Signal Design**

While moving average crossovers form the bedrock of macro trend identification, contemporary institutional systems augment these base signals with sophisticated overlays to improve risk-adjusted returns, mitigate volatility drag, and limit whipsaw friction.

### **1\. Ex-Ante Volatility-Scaled Position Sizing**

Similar to the user's validated crypto system, institutional macro systems universally utilize inverse volatility scaling prior to applying the trend signal. A market's position size is inversely proportional to its recent exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) volatility, typically utilizing a 60-day or 65-day lookback window16. This process normalizes the risk contribution across vastly different asset classes, ensuring that a highly volatile asset (like Natural Gas futures) contributes the exact same daily risk budget as a historically low-volatility asset (like the 2-Year US Treasury Note)17.

### **2\. Trend-Augmented Carry**

Many leading CTAs blend pure price momentum with fundamental carry signals. In fixed income markets, "trend" is often described by institutional managers as the art of safely collecting carry premia (the yield curve roll-down) while utilizing the trend signal as a risk-management overlay50. Allocating 15% to 20% of the portfolio's risk weight to FX and fixed-income carry signals provides a positive-expectancy return stream during sideways, range-bound markets, acting as a natural dampener when pure trend signals bleed capital12.

### **3\. Cross-Sectional Momentum and Spillover**

While time-series momentum looks at an asset in total isolation, modern systematic frameworks incorporate "network momentum" or momentum spillover51. This involves taking directional cues from highly correlated assets within the broader macro ecosystem. For example, a system might validate a short signal in the Canadian Dollar by analyzing whether crude oil trends are concurrently breaking down. This acts as a sophisticated regime filter, preventing the system from taking idiosyncratic trades that contradict the broader macroeconomic flow.

### **4\. Portable Alpha and Tail-Risk Overlays**

Recent portfolio construction research demonstrates that pairing trend-following with explicit tail-risk hedging creates a highly synergistic "portable alpha" portfolio17. By overlaying 30-delta, one-year put options held in staggered tranches alongside the trend portfolio, investors cover the strategy's primary blind spot. The put options protect against instantaneous "flash crashes" or massive gap-downs where trend-following is mathematically too slow to react, while the trend system protects against slow-bleed, multi-year bear markets where rolling option premiums become prohibitively expensive17.

## **Conclusions: The Illusion of Medium-Term Diversification**

Beyond the direct quantitative answers regarding parameterization and regime analysis, the single most profound insight uncovered in current academic and institutional research is the **mathematical redundancy of the medium-term trend horizon.**  
For decades, the managed futures industry operated under the heuristic assumption that blending fast (e.g., 1-month), medium (e.g., 3-to-6-month), and slow (e.g., 12-month) signals provided optimal time-scale diversification. The 60-to-120 day lookback window was universally heralded by practitioners as the irreplaceable "sweet spot" of trend following4.  
However, rigorous Bayesian graphical modeling published in late 2025 and 2026 proves that this practice creates a dangerous illusion of diversification33. The research demonstrates that the medium-term (125-day) signal is effectively a linear combination of the short-term and long-term trends34. By assigning equal weights to all three horizons, managers were unknowingly overweighting highly correlated signals, increasing portfolio complexity, amplifying transaction costs, and diluting signal efficacy without adding any unique predictive alpha.  
When the 125-day layer is stripped out completely, leaving only a "Barbell" of rapid reactivity (20-60 days) and structural persistence (250-500 days), the strategy's Sharpe ratio strictly improves and drawdown efficiency increases34.  
For a system architect transitioning from the hyper-fast digital asset space into traditional macro, this finding is revolutionary. It dictates that one does not need to hunt for the perfect "medium" speed to survive macroeconomic chop. Instead, the optimal approach is to construct a portfolio that explicitly separates the two realities of macroeconomic behavior—sudden panic and prolonged systemic shifts—and leave the noisy, mean-reverting middle entirely alone.
